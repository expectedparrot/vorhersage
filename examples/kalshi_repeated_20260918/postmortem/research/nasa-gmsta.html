GISTEMP GMSTA Analysis Project

Global Mean Surface Temperature Anomaly Predictions

This page shows various estimates of the current and next years' annual Global Mean Surface Temperature (GMST) anomaly for the GISS Surface Temperature Analysis (GISTEMP) v4 dataset, calculated with respect to the pre-industrial period 1850-1900. Conducted in partnership with the International Research Institute for Climate and Society (IRI) at Columbia University's Center for Climate Systems Research (CCSR), GMST anomaly predictions are generated from on a combination of North American Multi-Model Ensemble (NMME) forecast outputs, historical and projected December, January, February (DJF) El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) magnitudes, and a Year-to-Date (YTD) Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) bivariate regression model. The results are presented as a comparison of the different predictive methods, along with their associated confidence intervals. We provide an explanation of our methods and data sources, as well as a link to download the numerical prediction values shown in the plot below (Figure 1).

Figure 1: A comparison of GMST anomaly predictions for 2026 and 2027 based on NMME model outputs, historical and projected DJF ENSO magnitudes, and a YTD OLS bivariate linear regression. Data point error bars are prediction intervals and timeseries line shading represents confidence intervals, all within 95% certainty.

Methodologies and Sources

The anomaly with respect to 1850-1900 is calculated using the GISTEMP anomaly for 1880-1900, adjusted by the average of five other records (NOAA, HadCRUT5, Berkeley Earth, DCENT and China MST) to match the 1850-1900 period.

NMME Predictions

The North American Multi-Model Ensemble (NMME) is a collaborative project that combines the outputs of multiple state-of-the-art computer climate models, running in the United States and Canada, to improve the accuracy of seasonal and interannual climate predictions. Early each month, real-time configurations are used to generate temperature predictions for the upcoming 12-months. Because of this, rest-of-the-year temperature predictions tend to increase in accuracy with each passing month.

In this analysis, four NMME models are used to generate annual mean GMST anomaly predictions for this year. Those models are COLA-CCSM4, COLA-CESM1, CanESM5, and GEM5.2-NEMO. The predictions for each model are calculated by combining GISTEMPv4 anomalies for the observed portion of this year with the latest NMME rest-of-year forecast to calculate the full-year anomaly. NMME estimates are based on true air surface temperatures, whereas GISTEMP uses a conditional blend of air surface and sea surface temperatures. The confidence intervals around the NMME data represent the typical uncertainty spread for predictions released in the same issue month, based on historical accuracy.

Historical DJF ENSO Prediction

The Historical ENSO predictive model was developed through sequential residual fitting. This is an iterative process where statistical model residuals are used as dependent inputs in subsequent statistical models to improve accuracy. In this case, we used the Locally Estimated Scatterplot Smoothing (LOESS) method to build a local polynomial regression modeling smoothed annual GMST anomalies using data from GISTEMP v4. Then, we developed an OLS bivariate linear regression to model the relationship between LOESS residuals and historical DJF Niño 3.4 Sea Surface Temperature (SST) indices. We then constructed a final bivariate linear regression model, using LOESS GMST anomaly predictions + OLS residual predictions to explain observed historical GMST anomalies. Using the latest DJF Niño 3.4 SST index as an input within that final bivariate linear regression model, we can predict what the annual GMST anomaly of this year will be.

Year-To-Date Prediction

The YTD predictive model is an OLS bivariate linear regression that models the effect of historical YTD GMST anomalies on historical annual GMST anomalies. YTD values are calculated as the average of observed monthly GMST anomalies from the beginning of the year to the current month. Using the OLS bivariate model and the current YTD GMST anomaly, we can extrapolate what this year's annual GMST anomaly will be.

Projected DJF ENSO Prediction

The Future ENSO predictive model can be thought of as an extension of the historical ENSO predictive model, using its final bivariate linear regression to generate a prediction of what the annual GMST anomaly will be for next year. In this case, the previously developed OLS bivariate regression model is used to generate a prediction of residuals based on CCSR/IRI predictions of the upcoming DJF ENSO magnitude. We have chosen to use their predicted DJF dynamical mean and standard deviation for our calculations. Then, the previously developed LOESS polynomial regression model is used to extrapolate an annual GMST anomaly prediction for next year. Ultimately, those values are input as independent and dependent variables, respectively, to predict what the actual annual GMST anomaly will be next year.

Publication Notes

We are providing these estimates as a public service given the interest in the topic and frequent requests we get for this information. Updates to this page will be made on a monthly basis as the source data files get updated, usually by the middle of the month. We are working on a technical paper to be submitted on the verification and calibration of these estimates.
